The Quiet Return of the Lighthouse Keepers

Automation emptied the towers decades ago. Now a handful of volunteers are climbing the stairs again — for reasons that have little to do with ships.

By Marisol Ortega · March 14, 2025 · 9 min read

On a gray morning in February, Tomás Lindqvist unlocked a door that had not been opened since the winter storms. Inside, 308 iron steps spiral up to a lantern room where, for most of the twentieth century, someone sat awake every night of the year. “You don’t keep a light,” he told me, catching his breath at the top. “It keeps you.”

Lindqvist is one of about forty volunteers who now staff the station on weekends, giving tours, logging weather and — this is the part nobody expected — writing. The station's logbook, restarted in 2019, has grown to eleven volumes of observations, poems, and the occasional complaint about the wind.

A job that disappeared

The last civilian keeper left in 1975, when the lamp was fitted with a timer and a photocell. Across the country the story was the same: by 1990 every light but one had been automated. The keepers' houses were boarded up or sold, and the craft of tending a Fresnel lens — trimming wicks, polishing brass, winding the clockwork that turned the lens — passed out of living memory.

The keepers were never really guarding ships. They were guarding time: a promise that every night, at the same moment, the light would come on.

Historians at the maritime museum estimate that fewer than 200 people alive today ever did the work professionally. Several of them have been training the volunteers, who keep a checklist taped inside the watch room door:

  1. Record barometric pressure and wind at sunset.
  2. Check the lens for salt film; clean with distilled water only.
  3. Note every vessel sighted, with time and heading.
  4. Write something true in the log before leaving.

Why come back?

Ask the volunteers why they do it and the answers wander. Some came for the history; some for the view. Dana Whitfield, a retired schoolteacher, says she came for the silence: Up there the only notification is the weather. Others talk about the strange comfort of a routine that has not changed in a hundred and fifty years.

The program is not without critics. Conservation groups worry about foot traffic on the bluff, and the park service caps visits at 60 people a day. Still, the waiting list for weekend shifts is now longer than a year.

How to visit

  • Tours run Friday to Monday, 10 am to 4 pm, weather permitting.
  • The stairs are steep and narrow: allow 30 minutes.
  • Read the full visitor guide before you go.

As the light came on that evening — automatically, of course — Lindqvist wrote a single line in volume eleven: Wind NW, 22 knots. Two fishing boats heading home. All well.
